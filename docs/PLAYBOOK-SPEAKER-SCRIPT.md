## Slide 1 — AI Recommends the Product—and Points to the Seller

> Let me start with a concrete example.
>
> This is a summary of an actual Perplexity response supplied for this presentation. The shopping task concerns a lightweight, compact stroller for use in Portugal, within a budget of four hundred to seven hundred euros.
>
> Look at what the answer does.
>
> It does not simply return a list of websites. It selects three products, recommends one, explains the trade-offs and points to places to buy.
>
> Before the shopper enters a retailer's website, a shortlist and a preference have already been formed.
>
> For a retailer, that creates three different questions.
>
> Was my product considered?
>
> Was the information accurate?
>
> And was my shop offered as the seller?
>
> Those are not the same thing.
>
> This is one shopping-research response—not a visibility benchmark, a hands-on product test or an autonomous purchase.
>
> But it makes the challenge tangible: another interface is helping shape the decision before the retailer gets the opportunity to speak to the customer.

## Slide 2 — Agents, Assistants and New Decision Intermediaries

> There are three useful distinctions.
>
> First, research: finding and interpreting information for a customer's task.
>
> Second, recommendation: comparing options and suggesting which one best fits the request.
>
> Third, action: carrying out something the system has been permitted to do.
>
> Our Perplexity example demonstrates research and recommendation.
>
> The separate published checkout example on this slide describes a different capability, with its own participating sellers, market conditions and confirmation steps. It is not a continuation of the stroller session.
>
> I am not suggesting that every assistant can purchase every product in Portugal today.
>
> The point is that the retailer's job can no longer be framed only as attracting someone to a website and helping them navigate it.
>
> We also need to understand what happens before that visit: how products are interpreted, which options are presented and how the next step is selected.
>
> Rather than getting stuck on whether something is called an assistant or an agent, ask three practical questions:
>
> What information does it use? What decision does it influence? And what action is it allowed to take?

## Slide 3 — Visibility for Brands, Retailers and Platforms

> Visibility now needs to be examined from three perspectives.
>
> For the brand: is the product understood and considered for the customer's need?
>
> For the retailer: is our offer represented accurately, and are we included as a seller?
>
> For the platform: where are discovery, comparison and the next action happening—and what role does our own storefront play?
>
> The stroller example illustrates why these questions need to stay separate.
>
> A product can be recommended while a particular retailer is absent. Product specifications can come from one source and a purchase destination from another.
>
> A mention is therefore not the same as a visit, and a visit is not the same as a sale.
>
> My recommendation is to stop treating visibility as a single yes-or-no question.
>
> Ask what role your business plays in the answer. Then check whether the information is correct and whether the customer can take an appropriate next step.
>
> One observation will not tell you your position across the market. But it can reveal something specific worth investigating.

## Slide 4 — Who Controls Which Parts of the Journey?

> So who controls the recommendation: the brand, the retailer, the platform or the agent?
>
> The useful answer is that they control different parts.
>
> Brands and retailers can manage their own product information, offers and operating processes.
>
> The platform determines how its service selects and presents information.
>
> The shopper expresses the need and, where action is supported, provides the relevant authorisation.
>
> An agent should not automatically be treated as an independent actor that decides everything itself. We need to understand who operates it, which information it uses, which rules apply and what permissions it has.
>
> This distinction matters.
>
> Controlling your product data does not mean controlling the final recommendation.
>
> But not controlling the complete recommendation does not mean you are powerless.
>
> You can take responsibility for the accuracy of your information, the quality of your offer, the connections between your systems and your ability to deliver what you promise.
>
> That is where I would focus a retailer's effort: understand the boundaries, then improve the things you can actually own and operate.
>
> The diagram is an explanatory map—not a complete model of any platform's recommendation system.

## Slide 5 — Expertise First. AI Makes It More Effective.

> My recommendation is not to replace good experts with AI.
>
> It is to equip good experts to achieve more with it.
>
> In marketing, someone still needs to understand the customer, the positioning, the relevant product differences and the claims the business can substantiate.
>
> AI can help research, summarise, draft and adapt material. But the expert needs to recognise whether the result is accurate, distinctive and appropriate.
>
> In operations, someone needs to know the source of truth.
>
> If product information, price, availability or delivery details disagree, a fluent answer is not enough. Someone must understand the customer impact and what can safely be changed.
>
> AI can help compare records, describe a mismatch or prepare work. The operational responsibility still needs an owner.
>
> These are the capabilities I want retailers to develop: marketing expertise that can use AI effectively, and operations expertise that can use AI effectively.
>
> That does not necessarily mean creating two new departments.
>
> It means deciding who owns the task, what knowledge they need, where AI contributes and how the result will be evaluated.
>
> Start with competence. Add AI to a workflow with a purpose. Measure whether that combination improves the outcome.

## Slide 6 — Start with a Use Case. Build the Foundation to Scale It.

> People need a dependable foundation.
>
> Product information needs clear ownership and a reliable home.
>
> That is why I recommend assessing your product-information management capabilities: product identity, attributes, variants, suitability claims and the processes used to maintain them.
>
> Offer information—such as price, stock, delivery and returns—also needs a clear source and update process. Not everything has to live in one system.
>
> Next, assess how those systems exchange information and support actions.
>
> That is where APIs, feeds, permissions and controls become important.
>
> Do not begin with a complicated architecture diagram. Begin with a business task.
>
> What information does the task require? Where does that information live? How current must it be? Who may access it? What happens when it is wrong?
>
> You can start with existing systems while identifying where investment is needed.
>
> Starting today and investing in the foundation are not competing ideas.
>
> A PIM or API does not guarantee visibility. The objective is reliable information and controlled execution that can support more than a one-off experiment.

## Slide 8 — Start with One Bounded Use Case

> What can you do now?
>
> In the first week, choose one customer or operational task. Assign an expert owner, establish how it works today and choose a measure of success.
>
> Next, check the information and system access the task requires.
>
> Then test one bounded AI-assisted change, with appropriate review and a clear route for exceptions.
>
> Finally, assess the result—including what failed.
>
> Decide whether to repeat, stop, improve the data or invest in a connection or capability.
>
> A marketing team might start with a tightly defined product-content task. An operations team might start with identifying and resolving a recurring information mismatch.
>
> The visibility worksheet is another possible starting exercise—not the only one.
>
> My closing advice is this:
>
> Do not start by asking which AI product you should buy.
>
> Start by asking which customer task you want to improve, who takes responsibility and what information and capabilities they need.
>
> Start today—with expertise as the foundation, AI as an amplifier and systems that can support the next step.
>
> Once the public resources are ready, add:
>
> The Commonwork playbook gives you the examples, sources and a practical retailer guide to use with your team.

### Panel Debate — Introduce the Discussion

> To close, I’d like to put three statements to the panel. Each one connects the customer-facing opportunity to an operational choice retailers need to make.
>
> **Discovery & Data Governance:** If an AI agent cannot verify and trust your product facts in real time, your brand does not enter the consideration set.
>
> Are retailers over-investing in driving web traffic while under-investing in structured data and catalog governance?
>
> **Channel Control & Margin:** Having your product recommended is useless if the seller link defaults to a third-party marketplace instead of your store.
>
> Who captures the commercial value in agentic commerce: the brand, the retailer, or the interface platform?
>
> **Operating Model & Competence:** Winning in agentic commerce requires experts who know how to govern AI workflows—not just buying more tools.
>
> Do retailers need a complete platform replacement, or is the priority upskilling teams and connecting existing APIs?
>
> I’d invite each panelist to respond to the tension in those questions, then ask where they would focus first.
